Recommended iPad tool

Canva (Free · App Store / canva.com)

Canva is a drag-and-drop design platform that makes professional design principles accessible on iPad with no prior design software experience. It includes 1,000+ templates, a full color wheel, typography tools, grid layouts, and free asset libraries, all on a touch-friendly interface. Students can design, export, and share entirely on iPad.

Free alternatives: Adobe Express (free tier) · Google Slides (simple layouts) · Keynote (built into iPad, advanced typography tools)

Why this matters

Design is the silent language of every brand, poster, app, and social media post students interact with daily. When students understand design fundamentals (contrast, alignment, hierarchy, color, and typography), they stop being passive consumers and become intentional communicators. This lesson gives them a framework to critique the world around them and the tools to shape it.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  1. Identify and explain the 5 Fundamentals of Graphic Design: Contrast, Alignment, Repetition, Proximity, and Hierarchy (CARPH).
  2. Analyse real-world designs and critique them using design vocabulary.
  3. Apply all 5 fundamentals intentionally in an original graphic created in Canva on iPad.
  4. Evaluate a peer's design using a structured critique framework.
  5. Justify every design choice (font, color, layout, spacing) in relation to their target audience.
  6. Distinguish between a purposeful design and a visually cluttered one.

The 5 fundamentals of graphic design

C · A · R · P · H

These principles are the foundation of all visual communication, from logos to posters to websites. Post these on screen throughout the lesson.

C

Contrast

Make the important thing impossible to miss.

Contrast is the difference between elements: dark vs light, big vs small, bold vs thin, colorful vs neutral. Without contrast, everything competes equally for attention and nothing wins. Contrast creates visual hierarchy by telling the eye: "Start here." Example: a black headline on a white background has strong contrast; a grey headline on a light grey background does not.

A

Alignment

Create invisible order on the page.

Nothing should be placed randomly. Every element should have a visual connection to something else on the page. Alignment creates a sense of structure and professionalism; even subtle misalignment makes a design look amateur. Left-align blocks of text. Align images to a grid. Avoid centring everything: centred layouts are often the weakest for readability.

R

Repetition

Consistency builds trust and recognition.

Repeat visual elements (colors, fonts, shapes, line weights) throughout a design to create cohesion. Repetition is what makes a brand feel like a brand. On a single page, it unifies different sections so they feel like part of the same system. Example: the same font for all headings, the same icon style, and the same two colors everywhere.

P

Proximity

Group related things together.

Elements that are related should be physically close to each other; unrelated elements should be separated. Proximity reduces clutter, improves readability, and helps the viewer understand the structure without reading every word. Example: a photo and its caption should be close; the caption and the next section's heading should have more space between them.

H

Hierarchy

Guide the eye through a story.

Visual hierarchy tells the viewer what to look at first, second, and third. It comes from size (bigger is more important), color, weight (bold is more important), and position (top-left is read first in most cultures). A strong hierarchy means someone can glance at your design for 3 seconds and still understand the most important message.

The challenge

A real graphic for a real audience

“Design a real graphic for an actual school or community need, applying all 5 design fundamentals, that is so clear your audience understands the message in 3 seconds.”

Students choose one real context for their graphic:

Event poster

Design a poster for an upcoming school event, club, or program.

Social media card

Create an awareness post about a school rule, advocacy, or community issue.

Infographic card

Design a visual one-pager that explains a concept, process, or set of tips.

Brand identity card

Create a simple brand card for an imaginary school club, business, or NGO.

Your design must demonstrate all 5 fundamentals. You must be able to point to each one and explain why you made that choice for your specific audience.

60-minute lesson timeline

Minute by minute

Engage Investigate Act Reflect
  1. 0–7 minENGAGEDesign audit
    1 Gain attention3 Recall prior learning

    Teacher actions

    • Display 3 pairs of real-world graphics on screen: a cluttered flyer vs a clean event poster; a generic social media post vs a branded one; a hard-to-read sign vs a clear informational sign.
    • "For each pair: which one works better? Why?"
    • Take 4–5 responses. Don't give answers yet. Say: "Every answer you just gave has a name. Today you learn what they are."

    Student actions

    • Look at each pair and share their instinctive reaction.
    • Try to articulate WHY one design is stronger, using their own words.
    • Personal connection: think of a poster or ad that annoyed or confused you recently. What made it bad?
  2. 7–15 minENGAGEPresent the challenge
    2 Share the objective

    Teacher actions

    • Introduce the Big Idea: "Design is a language. Everyone can learn to speak it."
    • Reveal the Essential Question and The Challenge.
    • Show the 4 context options. Ask: "Which one feels most real and urgent to you?"
    • "Before you open Canva, you must know your audience and your message. Design without purpose is just decoration."

    Student actions

    • Choose their design context. Write their audience contract on paper: "My design is for ______. They need to feel ______ and do ______ after seeing it."
    • Share their choice with a partner for a 60-second reaction.
  3. 15–28 minINVESTIGATELearn the principles
    4 Present the content5 Guide the learning

    Teacher actions

    • Walk through all 5 fundamentals (C-A-R-P-H) using the slides or board.
    • For EACH principle: show a before/after visual example, name it, explain it in one sentence.
    • Guiding questions: "Where is the contrast in this design? What does it make you look at first?" · "Is this aligned to anything? What would happen if we moved it 10 pixels left?" · "Which elements repeat? Does that help or hurt?"
    • Quick activity: students annotate a printed or on-screen sample design, labelling where each of the 5 principles appears (or fails).

    Student actions

    • Write the 5 principles in their notebook with a one-sentence definition each.
    • Annotate the sample design with labels: C, A, R, P, H.
    • Rate the sample design 1–5 stars for each principle, and defend each rating in one sentence: "This design scores 2/5 for hierarchy because the headline and body text are nearly the same size."
  4. 28–30 minINVESTIGATESketch first
    5 Guide the learning6 Let them practice

    Teacher actions

    • Hard rule: "No one opens Canva until they have a rough sketch."
    • "Professionals sketch before they design. A 60-second thumbnail saves 10 minutes of wrong turns."
    • Show a teacher sketch example on the board, even a rough one.

    Student actions

    • Sketch a rough layout on paper or iPad Notes, 60 seconds maximum.
    • Mark: where is the headline? The image zone? The body text? The call to action?
    • Audience check: does this layout make sense for the person who will see it?
  5. 30–50 minACTBuild in Canva
    6 Let them practice

    Teacher actions

    • Set a visible timer. Keep the 5 principles and the success criteria on screen.
    • Circulate and ask principle-based guiding questions: "What creates the hierarchy here? Is the most important thing biggest?" · "I see 4 different fonts. What does Repetition say about that?" · "Your text and background are both light. What does Contrast say?" · "Why is this element floating? What can you align it to?"
    • At 44 min, announce: "6 minutes left. Make sure all 5 fundamentals are visible."

    Student actions

    • Build their graphic in Canva on iPad. Required: a clear headline with strong hierarchy · contrast between at least 2 elements · all elements aligned to a grid or to each other · consistent color and font (repetition) · related elements grouped closely (proximity) · a purpose statement visible in the design.
    • Extension: add a secondary information section, use brand colors, or create a second version for a different screen size.
  6. 50–56 minACTDesign critique
    7 Give feedback

    Teacher actions

    • Introduce the structured critique protocol: I Notice / I Wonder / I Suggest.
    • Demonstrate one round on a student example (with permission).
    • "Critique is not 'I like it' or 'it's ugly.' It is specific, principle-based feedback."

    Student actions

    • Pair share: swap iPads and use the protocol. I notice: one specific design choice I can name, using CARPH vocabulary. I wonder: one question about an intentional choice. I suggest: one specific, actionable improvement using a design principle.
    • Receive feedback, then make ONE refinement before sharing.
  7. 56–58 minREFLECTGallery and share
    8 Assess performance9 Help them transfer

    Teacher actions

    • Display 3–4 designs on screen (volunteers or selected).
    • "Explain one design decision you made using the name of the principle."
    • Ask the class: "Which principle was hardest to apply? Why?"
    • Connect to the real world: "If this poster went up in the hallway tomorrow, would it work?"

    Student actions

    • Briefly explain one principle-based decision in their design to the class.
    • Reflect: "My strongest principle is ___ because ___. My weakest is ___ because ___."
  8. 58–60 minREFLECTExit ticket
    8 Assess performance9 Help them transfer

    Teacher actions

    • Students export their design (PNG or PDF) from Canva.
    • Collect the exit ticket or have students submit it through Google Classroom.

    Student actions

    • (1) Name any ONE principle and show where it appears in your design.
    • (2) Name a design you see daily (an app, a sign, a brand) and identify one principle in it.
    • (3) Complete: "Before today I thought good design was ___. Now I think it's ___."

Success criteria · post on screen during the build

Must, should, could

Must have (all students)

  • Contrast: at least 2 clearly contrasting elements
  • Alignment: all elements aligned to a grid or to each other
  • Repetition: consistent font and color use (max 2 fonts, 3 colors)
  • Proximity: related content grouped, unrelated content separated
  • Hierarchy: the most important message is visually dominant
  • Real context: a specific audience is clearly identified
  • Can explain every design choice using principle vocabulary

Should have (most)

  • Visual tone matches the audience and topic
  • Typography: font choice reinforces the message
  • Design reads clearly in 3 seconds
  • Whitespace used intentionally (not filled randomly)

Could have (extension)

  • Two versions for different formats (story vs post)
  • Custom color palette with hex codes chosen deliberately
  • Icon set used consistently (repetition)
  • Design includes a clear call to action

Design critique protocol · reproducible for students

I notice · I wonder · I suggest

I notice

Something specific I can observe and name

I notice the ______ (element) uses ______ (principle).

This creates a feeling of ______.

The strongest principle applied is ______.

I wonder

A question about an intentional choice

I wonder why you chose ______ for the ______.

Did you consider ______ instead?

What audience were you thinking of when you chose ______?

I suggest

One specific, actionable improvement

To improve ______ (principle), you could ______.

If you increase / decrease / change ______, the ______ would improve.

This would help your audience ______.

Differentiation

Support, stretch, and tips

Support (scaffold)

  • Provide a pre-built Canva template with a correct grid layout; the student only changes content.
  • Give a printed CARPH checklist to mark off as they build.
  • Limit font choice to 2 pre-selected fonts and a 3-color palette.
  • Pair with a stronger student for the critique phase.

Extension (stretch)

  • Create two versions: one for print (A4 poster) and one for Instagram (1080×1080).
  • Build a mini brand guide: 2 fonts, 3 hex colors, and a rationale for each.
  • Critique two peer designs in writing using I Notice / I Wonder / I Suggest.
  • Research color psychology: why did they choose those specific colors for their audience?

Canva iPad tips

  • Tap the grid icon to turn on alignment guides; they snap elements into place.
  • Use Brand Kit (free) to lock colors and fonts for repetition.
  • Pinch to zoom in for precise alignment work.
  • Export as PNG (high quality) or PDF for printing.
  • Use Canva's color palette generator to build cohesive palettes.

Reflection prompts

Questions to close the loop

  1. Walk through your design and name the principle behind each major choice. Which one did you apply most confidently?
  2. Think of a brand, poster, or app you interact with daily. Now that you know CARPH, what do you notice about how it uses (or fails to use) these principles?
  3. What is the difference between a design that "looks nice" and a design that "communicates effectively"?
  4. If your target audience saw your design for only 3 seconds, what would they understand? Is that what you intended?
  5. What would you change if you had 30 more minutes? Which principle would you strengthen first, and why?
